What Is RAG? How AI Answers From Your Documents (Demo)

ChatGPT and other AI assistants sound convincing, but they do not know what is in your internal documents, and sometimes they confidently make things up. RAG is the most common way to fix that. In this article you do not just read the explanation, you try each stage yourself.

By Muhammad Muchson Attoyibi·October 23, 2026·4 min read

Key takeaways

  • RAG makes the AI read your documents before it answers, like an open-book exam instead of a memory test.
  • It works in five steps: split the documents, turn them into numbers, find the closest match, add it to the prompt, and let the model answer.
  • Chunk size and a clear "say you do not know" rule largely decide how good the answers are.
  • RAG lowers the risk of hallucination but does not remove it: show your sources and test the system.

The problem RAG solves

A language model (LLM) is trained on data collected up to a certain date. It does not know your school's policies, your shop's latest price list, or the contents of a PDF you received yesterday. When asked, it has two options: admit it does not know, or make up an answer that sounds right. The second is called a hallucination, and it is a real risk when AI is used for serious matters.

RAG stands for Retrieval-Augmented Generation, which roughly means "answering with the help of search". The idea is simple: before the model answers, the system searches first for the relevant pieces of your documents and pastes them into the question. The model is not asked to remember, but to read and summarise. Think of an open-book exam instead of a memory test.

Step 1: splitting documents (chunking)

A long document cannot be sent whole to the model for every question: it is too expensive, too slow, and the model struggles to focus. So the document is cut into small parts called chunks. How you cut it turns out to matter a great deal for answer quality. Try it below and notice what happens when chunks are too small or too large.

Rule of thumb

No single size is always right. Legal or policy text usually needs larger chunks so conditions and exceptions stay together. A list of questions and answers (FAQ) works well split by question. Start with roughly 200 to 500 words per chunk with a little overlap, then measure the result.

Steps 2 and 3: turning text into numbers, then searching

Computers do not "understand" sentences. Modern RAG systems turn each chunk of text into a list of numbers called an embedding. Texts with similar meanings produce numbers that sit close together. The user's question is also turned into an embedding, and the system looks for the closest chunks. This kind of search is called semantic search: the word "vehicle" can find a chunk that only mentions "car", even though no word is shared.

Test with a question that has no answer

Notice the fourth question (vegetarian canteen). The documents do not cover it, so a good system must answer "I could not find that information" instead of making something up. That is why the instruction in the prompt matters.

Steps 4 and 5: the prompt and the answer

Once the best chunks are chosen, the system builds a prompt that reads roughly: "Answer only from the documents below. If the answer is not there, say you do not know." followed by the chunks and the question. The model then writes the answer. Because the source chunks are known, the system can show quotes or links to the sources, so users can check for themselves.

Why RAG is often the first choice

  • Content can be updated without retraining the model. Replace a document, the index updates, done.
  • Answers can be traced back to their sources, so they are easier to trust and audit.
  • Your data stays with you. Internal documents do not need to go into model training.
  • Lower cost than training or tuning your own model for needs that are mostly about knowledge.

Common mistakes when building RAG

Table 1: Common mistakes when building RAG
ProblemSymptomFix
Chunks too large or too smallIncomplete or off-target answersTest several sizes, add overlap
Messy or outdated source documentsWrong answers that still "look" supported by a sourceClean up documents and date them
Relying on one kind of searchProduct names or codes are not foundCombine keyword and semantic search (hybrid)
No "I do not know" ruleThe AI makes things up when documents are emptyRequire refusal in the prompt, test with trick questions
Never measuredQuality drops without anyone noticingPrepare 30 to 50 test questions and score them regularly

Something often forgotten: security

The contents of documents go into the prompt. If a document contains a sentence like "ignore the previous instructions", the model can be fooled. This is called prompt injection. Treat document contents as data, limit what the model is allowed to do (for example, read only, never send email), and do not mix confidential documents from different users in one index without access control.

FAQ

What is the difference between RAG and fine-tuning?
Fine-tuning changes a model's behaviour or style through extra training. RAG gives the model reading material at the moment it answers. For knowledge that changes often, RAG is usually more practical and cheaper.
Does RAG eliminate hallucinations?
Not completely. RAG lowers the risk because answers are anchored in documents, but the model can still misread or misreport them. That is why sources should be shown and the system should be tested.
Do I need a vector database?
For a few dozen documents, simple search is often enough. A vector database starts to pay off when you have thousands of chunks or search has to be very fast.
Which documents suit RAG?
Internal guides, FAQs, policies, training material and product documentation. Well-organised, up-to-date documents produce much better answers.
